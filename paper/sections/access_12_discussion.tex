\textit{What no level hides.} Encryption hides content, not length. Frame sizes reveal motion at every level alike (Fig.~\ref{fig:size}). They also identify a known video, as Schuster et al.\ showed for encrypted streaming~\cite{schuster2017}: an attacker who encodes candidate videos himself and correlates the size sequence picks the right one of \FpVideos{} videos from ten frame sizes \FpEtwoTen\,\% of the time under E1--E4 and \FpEfiveTen\,\% under E5 (chance \FpChance\,\%, Table~\ref{tab:fingerprint}). Padding to 1\,KB steps lowers that to \FpKbTen\,\% for $+$\FpKbCost\,\% bytes but not over 100 frames (\FpKbHundred\,\%), padding to the running maximum to \FpRunTen\,\% for $+$\FpRunCost\,\%, and only constant-size frames reach chance, at $+$\FpConstCost\,\% on these edited clips. Constant-size frames close the channel cheaply for a fixed camera ($+$\PadFixed\,\%) but cost up to $+$\PadFilmMax\,\% bytes on edited clips; padding to the largest frame so far gives a separability of \PadRunSep{} for $+$\PadRunCost\,\% on the fixed camera (\PadRunFilmSepMin--\PadRunFilmSepMax{} on the real clips), while bitrate regulation lets up to \RcPadOverMax\,\% of frames exceed a cap. A surveillance camera is the favourable case: its scene is fixed and padding is cheap. The link ships the running-maximum rule as an option: the padding is a comment segment after the start-of-image marker, which every decoder skips and the tag does not need to cover, so a padded frame verifies and decodes exactly like the unpadded one (\texttt{link/wire.py}, \texttt{tests/test\_padding.py}).

\textit{Limitations.} Five limitations bound our claims. First, the board confirms correctness but not yet speed or power: the encoder is bit-exact on the PYNQ-Z2, but our frame rate there is set by a Python driver on the ARM, the fabric-only time and the power are not yet measured on the board, and the power figures come from simulated switching activity; whether DDR feeds the four-lane encoder its \RtlFourLanesInMBs\,MB/s of pixels is also open. Second, sign and amplitude encryption leak outlines by construction (Lemma~\ref{lem:format}), however many bits are encrypted; only a change of block positions removes them, at a bitrate cost. Third, frame sizes leak at every level, as above. Fourth, our attackers are greedy or trained on a few clips; stronger puzzle solvers or attackers trained on large video corpora may extract more from E5, and a trained network already undoes sign-only encryption (E1). Fifth, a pre-shared key must still be provisioned. It no longer encrypts anything itself: at session start the processing system runs a hybrid exchange, the module-lattice KEM ML-KEM-768 of FIPS~203~\cite{fips203} together with X25519, authenticated with a key derived from the pre-shared one, and the session's master key is derived from both shared secrets and the transcript, much as DTLS-SRTP keys SRTP~\cite{rfc3711}. A recorded session therefore stays secret if the pre-shared key leaks later or a quantum computer breaks the elliptic curve, and a forged or replayed message fails its authentication (\texttt{link/kex.py}, \texttt{tests/test\_pq\_kex.py}); its time on the Cortex-A9 is \TODO{A3: experiments/pq\_kex\_cost.py on the board}. The fabric is untouched; it just receives a different 128-bit key. The fabric itself needs no change, because Ascon-AEAD128 is symmetric: Grover's search~\cite{grover1996} needs on the order of $2^{64}$ evaluations of the cipher to find a 128-bit key, which is a reduction of the security margin, not a break. On the protocol side, the receiver cannot tell heavy benign loss from partial jamming, so only near-total suppression escalates, and the reaction time is bounded below by the one-second decision window.

\textit{Inspirations, and their limits.} Several design choices follow vision systems. The lanes copy the retina's parallel pathways, each of which sees part of the image and is merged later; change-driven coding (Section~\ref{sec:further}) copies the retina's and event cameras' preference for change over repetition, and its measured cost shows where that analogy stops (cheap on a fixed camera, expensive on edited footage). A compound eye of many independent facets is what the restart segments are: each decodes alone, which makes both the lanes and the E5 permutation possible. A fovea suggests the opposite of uniform protection, strong encryption only where faces or plates are; we leave that region-of-interest policy open, because a detector on the camera is itself a target. The split between programmable logic and processing system mirrors the one between retina and cortex: the fabric does the fixed per-pixel work at line rate, the processor the per-frame decisions (headers, permutation order, policy, keys), and the same encoder in C on the processor (Section~\ref{sec:board}) measures what the fabric adds. Cameras suggested the input format: a sensor's processor produces YCbCr and only converts to RGB for display, so a lane encoder built for YUYV input (Section~\ref{sec:lanes}) takes the camera's bytes as they are, four pixels per beat and half the bytes per pixel. Electronics suggested the timing work: a long on-chip wire is cut by repeaters, and our long handshake chains are cut by two-entry register slices; and the lanes are interleaved like the phases of a multiphase converter, each taking every $L$-th macroblock row at a fraction of the total rate.

\textit{Reproducibility and data.} Every number in this paper is produced by a script from stored results and inserted into the text by a generator, never typed; a test fails if a table in the manuscript differs from what the data produce. The open sources (the Blender Foundation films and the Kodak images) can be downloaded and rerun; the private clips are identified by SHA-256 hash and never shown, and frames of them are never stored as images. The golden model, the RTL, the testbenches, the Vivado and board scripts, the experiment scripts and the scripts that rerun all GPU and long-CPU experiments in one cloud session are available at \url{https://github.com/bnsama29-cloud/FPGA-Video-Encryption-}.

\textit{Lessons.} Three kinds of error recurred while building this system, and each changed how we work. Simulation-only success hid timing problems that only place and route exposed, so post-route numbers are now part of every hardware claim. Experiment scripts were wrong more often than the RTL: a set point that clips opening on black could not reach, an estimator that wound up when its input saturated, a controller tuned at one operating point and unstable at another, and a test source that stamped the wall-clock time into every frame. Each was found because a result looked implausible on new data, and each now has a regression test. Finally, two claims had to be withdrawn when stronger evidence arrived: the E5 edge correlation of the U-Net is above that of the plain prior once more data is used, which the frame-statistics control explains, and the cost of hiding frame sizes is small only for fixed cameras.

\textit{Ethics.} The clips with identifiable people are used only to measure what an eavesdropper could recover; no picture derived from them appears here, and none is stored.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Encrypting coefficient signs and amplitudes with Ascon inside a JPEG encoder costs no cycles, provably keeps every protection level playable, and, combined with a per-frame tag over metadata and scan, turns a plain MJPEG stream into an authenticated one. Field encryption alone, however, leaves the outline of the scene to anyone who counts non-zero coefficients, and published format-compliant schemes also fall to one known frame because their key material does not change. A keyed permutation of macroblocks that is drawn anew for every frame removes the outline under every attack we could build, and with restart segments it fits in hardware without a frame buffer: the fabric codes and authenticates the segments, the processor only reorders them. Parallel lanes over the same segments raise throughput to \RtlFourLanesMpix\,Mpixel/s at 100\,MHz, and a closed feedback loop answers active network attacks within about a second in simulation. No level hides frame sizes, which identify a known video and reveal motion; constant-size frames close that channel at a cost that is small only for fixed cameras. Next are the fabric-only time and power measured on the board, the processor baseline on the same chip, size padding in hardware, a shared Ascon core across lanes, a registered quantiser table for clocks above 125\,MHz in the complete system, and key derivation with the standard's extendable-output function.
