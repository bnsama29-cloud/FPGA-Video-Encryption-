\newcommand{\PaperTitle}{Playable but Private: In-Encoder Selective Encryption with Ascon-AEAD128 and Attack-Adaptive Protection for MJPEG Video on an FPGA SoC}
\newcommand{\PaperAbstract}{Small video cameras increasingly stream to networks they do not control, yet encrypting the whole stream makes it unplayable without the key and spends energy on every byte. We present a JPEG/MJPEG encoder for a Zynq-7020 FPGA SoC that encrypts only the sign and amplitude bits of selected DCT coefficients, at the entropy-coding stage and with a keystream from the NIST SP~800-232 lightweight standard Ascon-AEAD128. Huffman code lengths are untouched, so every protection level remains a valid JPEG stream, while a 128-bit tag computed over the frame metadata and the coded scan lets the receiver reject any modified, replayed or forged frame. The register-transfer-level design is verified bit-exact against a Python golden model and needs no additional clock cycles for encryption in any mode. Around the encoder we build an authenticated feedback channel, a key ratchet and an adaptive policy that raises protection when the receiver reports attacks. In a networked simulation, all \NumAttacks{} active attacks we model were mitigated in \TtmMean{}\,s on average with no false alarms, and on \NumVideoClips{} video clips and the Kodak images a keyed block shuffle removed the outline leakage that sign and amplitude encryption alone leave behind, also against a jigsaw solver and a trained neural attacker; the same network defeats sign-only encryption. We also report a negative result: a learning bandit policy saves \PolCostSave{}\,\% of encrypted bits but reacts more slowly than a fixed threshold rule.}

\section{Introduction}
Cameras on doors, corridors and machines now send video over Wi-Fi and shared Ethernet as a matter of course. Two properties matter for such a link. Confidentiality keeps a bystander on the network from watching the scene; integrity lets the viewer trust that what it shows was captured by the camera and not edited, replayed or injected on the way. The usual answer, encrypting the transport with TLS, SRTP~\cite{rfc3711} or a VPN, provides both, but it hides the stream from every tool that is not part of the key infrastructure and costs cipher work proportional to the full bitrate.

Selective encryption takes a different route: it encrypts only the parts of the compressed representation that carry most of the visual information, and leaves the format intact so that standard decoders still parse the result~\cite{massoudi2008}. For JPEG, the sign and appended amplitude bits of the DCT coefficients can be encrypted without changing any Huffman code length, so the bitstream stays compliant and its size does not grow~\cite{vandroogenbroeck2002,auer2013}. The price is that such schemes are easy to weaken: the positions of non-zero coefficients stay visible, and an attacker who guesses the hidden values can still see outlines~\cite{massoudi2008,lin2024}.

This work starts from a practical question: how much of a secure camera link can be put into the encoder hardware itself, and what does an eavesdropper still learn? We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item An MJPEG encoder in synthesizable Verilog in which an Ascon-AEAD128~\cite{sp800232,ascon} keystream is XORed onto coefficient signs and amplitudes as they are entropy-coded, with no added cycles and closing timing at 100\,MHz on a Zynq-7020, a parallel-lane variant over JPEG restart segments that reaches \RtlFourLanesMpix\,Mpixel/s at the same clock, and a per-frame tag that binds the coded scan to the protection level, session, frame counter and image size (Section~\ref{sec:design}).
\item Six protection levels, from tag-only to a keyed shuffle of macroblocks, evaluated against five keyless attackers (value replacement, the sign-recovery program of Lin et al., a jigsaw solver, a trained U-Net and a chosen-plaintext attack) on a synthetic scene, \NumVideoClips{} real video clips (\NumOpenClips{} of them openly licensed) and the Kodak image set, with the cost of restart intervals and the frame-size channel that no level hides (Sections~\ref{sec:security} and~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\item An authenticated feedback loop with a forward-secure key ratchet and an adaptive policy, evaluated against \NumAttacks{} active attacks and benign impairments; a threshold rule is compared with a contextual bandit over \Seeds{} paired seeds (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
\item A verification flow in which a Python golden model is the reference for every stage: Ascon matches all official known-answer tests, and every RTL block matches the model bit for bit.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\textit{Selective encryption of JPEG.} Van Droogenbroeck and Benedett encrypted the appended bits of selected AC coefficients of baseline JPEG\ifextended, leaving the Huffman code words in place,\fi{} so that the stream stays compliant at a constant bit rate~\cite{vandroogenbroeck2002}. Puech and Rodrigues encrypted selected DCT coefficients of medical images with AES\ifextended{} in OFB mode\fi~\cite{puech2005}, and Auer et al.\ encrypted baseline JPEG at bitstream level\ifextended, permuting Huffman code-word/value pairs and toggling value bits\fi{} with an AES-OFB generator at more than 25 VGA frames per second, preserving length and format~\cite{auer2013}. Massoudi et al.\ survey the field and note that visual distortion is subjective and does not measure security: replacement and chosen-plaintext attacks recover more than the scrambled image suggests~\cite{massoudi2008}. Lin et al.\ show that DCT sign bits can be recovered by optimization~\cite{lin2024}, which bounds what sign-only encryption can promise. Hirose et al.\ analyse JPEG encryption that shuffles blocks delimited by restart markers~\cite{hirose2024}; our strongest level uses the same mechanism with a keyed permutation. Li et al.\ recover block-shuffled JPEG with a designed plaintext that reveals the permutation\ifextended{} through its per-block non-zero counts, which then inverts any image shuffled with the same permutation\fi~\cite{li2025shuffle}. Selective encryption has also been extended to H.264/AVC and HEVC~\cite{boyadjis2017}.

\textit{Hardware.} Kishore et al.\ added PN-sequence block and symbol scrambling to an FPGA JPEG encoder\ifextended{} for 256$\times$256 grey images on Stratix~II and~IV; they describe the extra hardware as about 1\,\%, while their tables show\else; their tables show\fi{} 13--17\,\% more ALUTs\ifextended, twice the memory bits\fi{} and 9--10\,\% fewer frames per second with it~\cite{kishore2015}, and Ba\u{g}baba and \"Ors combined an FPGA JPEG encoder with TEA encryption of the DC\ifextended{} coefficients, or of the DC and first five AC coefficients, of every block\else{} and first five AC coefficients\fi~\cite{bagbaba2015}. Neither authenticates the frames. Ascon, now standardized by NIST in SP~800-232~\cite{sp800232,ascon}, is small and fast in hardware (about 7\,kGE for a core with one unrolled round)~\cite{gross2017}; Gladis Kurian and Chen implement Ascon on an Artix-7 with replay detection through a Bloom filter in block RAM\ifextended, which has no false negatives but a small false-positive rate (0.17\,\% measured)\fi~\cite{kurian2025}.

\textit{Traffic analysis.} Sizes and timing of encrypted streams reveal motion~\cite{wampler2015} and which video is played~\cite{schuster2017}; traffic shaping hides IoT activity at up to $18\times$ the bandwidth for a camera~\cite{apthorpe2019}.

\textit{Gap.} We found no design that (i)~places a standardized lightweight AEAD inside a hardware JPEG encoder at the entropy-coding stage, (ii)~keeps every protection level playable while authenticating each frame together with its metadata, and (iii)~closes the loop from the receiver back to the encoder so that the protection level follows observed attacks. We also could not find an evaluation of the outline leakage of such schemes against learned attackers on real video.

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
\subsection{Baseline JPEG entropy coding}
Baseline JPEG~\cite{t81,wallace1992} converts each 16$\times$16 macroblock (MCU) of a 4:2:0 frame into six 8$\times$8 blocks (four of luma, two of chroma), applies the two-dimensional DCT and divides every coefficient by an entry of a quantisation table. The quantised coefficients are read in zig-zag order. The first, DC, coefficient is coded as the difference to the previous block of the same component; the 63 AC coefficients are coded as pairs (run of zeros, size category) followed by the coefficient's value. The pair goes into a Huffman code; the value is appended as a raw field of exactly \emph{size} bits, in one's-complement form for negative numbers. Two properties follow and our design rests on both. First, the appended field can be changed without changing the Huffman code or the stream length, so a cipher applied to it keeps the stream decodable. Second, the Huffman symbols, and with them the run lengths and size categories of every non-zero coefficient, stay visible to anyone; they carry the outline of the picture: the number of non-zero coefficients per block alone recovers its contours (the non-zero counting attack applied in~\cite{hirose2024}), which is why sign and amplitude encryption alone cannot hide it. A restart marker resets the DC prediction and delimits a segment that a decoder can process on its own, which is what makes the block shuffle of E5 and the parallel lanes of Section~\ref{sec:further} possible.

\subsection{Ascon-AEAD128}
Ascon~\cite{ascon}, standardised by NIST in SP~800-232~\cite{sp800232}, is a family of lightweight permutation-based schemes. Ascon-AEAD128 takes a 128-bit key and a 128-bit nonce, absorbs associated data and message in 128-bit blocks into a 320-bit state with the 12-round or 8-round Ascon permutation, and returns the ciphertext and a 128-bit tag. The permutation consists of a constant addition, a 5-bit S-box layer and a linear diffusion layer, all of which map to few logic levels in hardware~\cite{gross2017}. We use the construction in two ways: as a keystream generator (the ciphertext of an all-zero message under the frame's encryption nonce) and as a MAC over the frame's metadata and coded scan (the tag of an empty message with the scan as associated data, under a separate nonce). Both reuse one permutation core design; the RTL instantiates two copies so that keystream and tag are produced in parallel with the encoder.

\subsection{Threat model}
The attacker sits on the network path. It may observe all traffic, and it may modify, drop, delay, replay or inject both video and feedback datagrams; it does not hold the keys. A passive eavesdropper sends nothing, so it cannot be detected; against it only the configured minimum protection level helps. Physical and side-channel attacks on the device are out of scope\ifextended; a deployment that needs them can use a threshold implementation of Ascon, which Gro{\ss} et al.\ built at about 3.1 times the area of their unprotected low-area core~\cite{gross2017}\fi. The assets are the visual content of each frame, the integrity of content and metadata (protection level, session, counter and size), and the availability signal that tells the viewer the camera is alive. A (key, nonce) pair never repeats: the 63-bit frame counter only increases within a run, every rekey changes both the key and the session identifier, and every boot draws a fresh 48-bit random session base\ifextended\ (Section~\ref{sec:nonce})\fi.

\ifextended
\subsection{Nonce uniqueness, rekeying and reboots}\label{sec:nonce}
A stream cipher fails catastrophically if a (key, nonce) pair is used twice, since the XOR of two ciphertexts then reveals the XOR of the plaintexts. Our nonce has 128 bits: a 64-bit session identifier and a 63-bit frame counter, plus one purpose bit that separates the keystream nonce from the tag nonce of the same frame. The session identifier holds a 48-bit random base and a 16-bit key epoch. Uniqueness then follows from four rules, which the sender enforces and the receiver checks. (i) Within a run, the frame counter starts at one and strictly increases; the encoder does not check it, so the driver on the ARM core must never write a value twice, and the receiver's replay window discards repeats. (ii) Every rekey advances the ratchet, which changes the video key, and the epoch, which changes the session identifier; a frame from a new epoch therefore uses a new key even if its counter value occurred before. (iii) Every boot draws a fresh 48-bit base from the operating system's random source, so two runs share a session identifier only by chance, with probability $2^{-48}$ for a given pair of runs; a deployment that fixes the base in its configuration must persist the counter across reboots instead. (iv) The key-derivation function uses session identifier zero, which no frame can carry because the base is never zero. When the 16-bit epoch field wraps, the session identifier repeats but the ratchet has produced a different key, so no (key, nonce) pair repeats.
\fi

\subsection{Encoder and cipher integration}
Fig.~\ref{fig:system} shows the datapath of a baseline JPEG encoder~\cite{t81,wallace1992}: colour conversion with 4:2:0 subsampling, a two-pass integer DCT, a reciprocal-multiplication quantiser, zig-zag ordering with run-length coding, the encryption stage, Huffman coding and a bit packer. The whole datapath is fixed-point and mirrors the golden model operation for operation, so the hardware output can be compared byte by byte.

The keystream generator runs Ascon-AEAD128 on an all-zero message under the frame nonce, one permutation round per clock cycle, and keeps a small buffer of keystream bits ahead of the encryption stage. For each coefficient the encryption stage consumes keystream bits only for the fields the protection level selects. If the sign is selected and the next keystream bit is one, all bits of the coefficient's appended amplitude field are inverted; in JPEG's one's-complement amplitude coding this is exactly a sign change. If amplitudes are selected, the remaining bits are XORed onto the lower bits of the field. The size category, and therefore the Huffman code, is never changed. As a result the encrypted scan has the same length as the plain one, and the keystream generator works in parallel with the rest of the pipeline.

A second Ascon instance computes the tag. Its associated data is a 26-byte prefix (format identifier, version, protection level, 64-bit session identifier, 64-bit frame counter, width and height) followed by every byte of the coded scan. The ARM core adds standard JPEG headers and an APP11 segment that carries the prefix and the tag; ordinary viewers skip this segment. Binding the level into the tag matters: an earlier version did not, and a frame could be downgraded undetected.

The 128-bit nonce concatenates the 64-bit session identifier with the 63-bit frame counter and one purpose bit that separates the keystream from the tag computation. The session identifier holds 48 random bits and the current key epoch.

\subsection{Protection levels}
Every level carries the per-frame tag. E0 encrypts nothing (authentication only), E1 the AC sign bits, E2 also the DC-difference signs (default minimum), E3 also the AC amplitude bits, E4 also the DC amplitude bits, and E5 adds a keyed permutation of 16$\times$16 macroblocks. Sign and amplitude encryption cannot hide which coefficients are non-zero, because the Huffman symbols reveal run lengths and size categories. E5 therefore inserts a restart marker after every macroblock and permutes the resulting segments with a Fisher--Yates shuffle driven by Ascon under a separately derived key. Restart markers keep the stream decodable but cost \OverheadEfiveQVGA--\OverheadEfiveVGA\,\% bitrate over E4 in our measurements (320$\times$240 and 640$\times$480), whereas E2 adds only the \mbox{38-byte} APP11 segment (\OverheadEtwoVGA--\OverheadEtwoQVGA\,\%). E5 is implemented in the golden model; restart-marker support in the RTL is ongoing work.

\subsection{Feedback, keys and adaptive protection}
The receiver authenticates every frame, checks the counter against a 64-entry replay window as in SRTP~\cite{rfc3711}, and returns heartbeats every 0.5\,s and rate-limited alerts. Feedback messages are themselves Ascon-AEAD128 encrypted under a separate key and protected by their own replay window. The video key is advanced by a one-way ratchet, $K_{e+1}=\mathrm{KDF}(K_e,\textrm{``ratchet''},e{+}1)$, so a key captured later does not expose earlier epochs. A dead-man rule treats three seconds without a valid heartbeat as an attack: the sender switches to the highest level and rekeys.

The sender's ARM core collects evidence in one-second windows: tag failures, replays, malformed or stale frames, frame loss over three windows, forged feedback and unexpected traffic. Two policies map this evidence to a protection level. The \emph{threshold} policy escalates to the highest level on strong evidence, holds it for 8\,s, and rekeys on bursts of tag failures. The \emph{bandit} policy is an $\varepsilon$-greedy contextual bandit~\cite{sutton2018} over a small discrete context, with optimistic initial values and sample-average updates; its reward penalizes leakage weighted by the evidence of attack, encrypted bits and level switches. Neither policy goes below the configured minimum level.

\ifextended
\textit{Pipeline.} The encoder is a chain of stages joined by valid/ready handshakes: colour conversion and 4:2:0 subsampling into MCU-row buffers, a DCT that accepts one sample per clock, a quantiser built from multiplications by reciprocal tables, zig-zag ordering with run-length coding, the selective-encryption stage, the Huffman coder, a bit packer and a word packer to the AXI-Stream output. Every stage holds its data while the next one is not ready, so back-pressure from the output never corrupts the stream. The keystream generator fills a small buffer in parallel; the encryption stage takes only the bits the current coefficient needs, so the pipeline never waits for the cipher.

\textit{Timing closure.} Three lessons from the Vivado runs are worth recording. The first implementation met timing in simulation but reached only about 47\,MHz after routing, because the DCT summed a full row in one clock; splitting the sums over five pipeline stages with a stall-able handshake restored 100\,MHz without changing an output bit. The second appeared only in the complete Zynq system: the DMA's output register fed the colour conversion, clamp and line-buffer write in one clock. Registering the pixel, the multiply-add sums and the clamped result moved the writes three clocks later in the same order. The third came with the lanes: the zig-zag stage, which formed the zig-zag address, read the block buffer, took the DC difference and derived the size category and amplitude bits in one clock, had been the critical path of the single pipeline, with tens of picoseconds of slack, and missed 100\,MHz in the two-lane system. Storing coefficients in zig-zag order and splitting the coding into two handshaked stages closed timing for every configuration and raised the encoder's slack to $+$\TopWns\,ns; at 125\,MHz the \TopLimitAtOneTwoFive{} is now the limit (WNS \TopWnsAtOneTwoFive\,ns). In all three cases the golden model was the referee, so the fix was accepted only when every RTL test still matched it bit for bit.

\textit{Verification method.} Each RTL block has a cocotb testbench that drives random inputs with random back-pressure and compares every output with the golden model, reporting the first mismatch with its index, input and expected value. Every defect found this way, in the RTL, the model or the experiment scripts, received a regression test and an entry in a bug log with its root cause and fix. The Ascon model passes the official known-answer tests of the standard, and the complete encoder is checked byte for byte at the top level, in Icarus Verilog and in Vivado's simulator.

\section{Security Evaluation}\label{sec:security}
\textit{Setup.} We measure what a keyless observer can reconstruct. For every frame we take the better of two pictures: the protected stream decoded as is, and a \emph{replacement attack} that substitutes fixed guesses for the encrypted values (absolute values for encrypted signs, the smallest value of the size category for encrypted amplitudes, zero DC differences) and re-encodes a valid JPEG. Leakage is the Pearson correlation between the Sobel gradient magnitudes of this picture and of the original (edge correlation); we also report SSIM~\cite{ssim}. Sources are a synthetic corridor scene; \NumClips{} clips that we analyse but do not reproduce (two film trailers, two talking-head videos and a handheld phone video, identified by hash); \NumOpenClips{} openly licensed films of the Blender Foundation, the Sintel trailer and Tears of Steel (CC~BY~3.0)\footnote{\url{https://durian.blender.org}, \url{https://mango.blender.org}}; and the 24 images of the Kodak set\footnote{\url{http://r0k.us/graphics/kodak/}}. \section{Protocol and Defence Evaluation}\label{sec:protocol}
\textit{Setup.} Sender, network channel, receiver and logger run as separate processes that talk over UDP, so the evaluation exercises the real wire format, fragmentation and timing. The sender encodes 320$\times$240 video at 10 frames/s with the golden model (same bytes as the RTL). The channel can impose loss, delay, jitter and reordering, and it implements the attacker of our threat model. All runs used a 4-core cloud virtual machine.

\textit{Active attacks.} The full scenario applies \NumAttacks{} attacks for six seconds each, 25\,s apart: bit flips in the scan, a mode downgrade in the header, splicing of two frames, injection of a forged frame, replay, delay, dropping, a junk flood, and dropping, forging and replaying feedback. Table~\ref{tab:attacks} gives the result over \SuiteRuns{} runs. Every attack was mitigated in every run, on average \TtmMean{}\,$\pm$\,\TtmSd{}\,s after it began, with \SuiteFalse{} false alarms in calm windows and a median frame latency of \SuiteLatency\,ms. The slowest case, dropped feedback, is caught only by the dead-man rule, after about \FbDropTtm\,s: the 3\,s timeout plus part of a decision window. With a trailer, a talking-head clip or Tears of Steel as the video source instead of the synthetic scene, all attacks were again mitigated (\RealTrailerMit{}, \RealTalkMit{} and \RealOpenMit{}; means \RealTrailerTtm{}, \RealTalkTtm{} and \RealOpenTtm{}\,s, one run each) without false alarms.

\textit{Benign impairments.} A second scenario applies only network problems: rising packet loss up to 6\,\%, delay up to 120\,ms with jitter, and 5\,\% reordering. Over \BenignRuns{} runs the defence raised no false alarm (\BenignFalse{} windows); median latency was \BenignLatency\,ms and the 95th percentile \BenignPninetyfive\,ms.\ifextended This needed loss to be judged over three windows and late frames to be told apart from replays.\fi

\textit{Peculiar case 1: the evidence decides the reaction time.} The attacks of Table~\ref{tab:attacks} are all mitigated, but Fig.~\ref{fig:caseevidence} shows that they leave three different kinds of evidence, and the evidence, not the harm, sets how fast the defence can react. A bit flip is the easy case: the receiver rejects the first tampered frame after \CaseBitflipFirstRej\,s on average over \CaseRuns{} runs and the sender reaches E5 after \CaseBitflipAware\,s. Holding frames back produces no rejection while it lasts, because nothing arrives at all. When the next frame gets through, the receiver counts the gap as lost frames; the held frames, released after the \CaseDelayDuration\,s attack, still carry valid tags but are older than frames already shown, so they are discarded as late (\CaseDelayRejected{} per run, the first \CaseDelayFirstRej\,s after the attack began), the verdict our replay window gives to benign reordering. The defence therefore never sees a delay: it reacts after \CaseDelayAware\,s to video that stops while heartbeats still arrive, which it treats as jamming. Silencing the feedback path is the mirror image. The receiver sees perfect video and rejects \CaseFbdropRejected{} frames, yet the sender, which learns about the link only through authenticated heartbeats, goes blind, and only the dead-man rule escalates, after \CaseFbdropAware\,s. The stream stays at its floor level for longest during this attack, which gives it the highest leakage of all attacks while active (\CaseFbdropLeak, the mean edge correlation of the active level). Integrity attacks are caught at the first frame; attacks by absence can only be caught by a timeout, so their reaction time is a design parameter, and shortening it trades against false alarms on a slow network.

\textit{Parallel lanes.} Restart markers reset DC prediction, so segments can be coded by independent lanes and concatenated. On all \LaneSources{} sources the merged output of 2--4 lanes is byte-identical to the sequential encoder; one segment per MCU row costs \RestartRowMin--\RestartRowMax\,\% bitrate. A single keystream per frame would serialize the lanes, so the model derives a key per frame and a nonce per segment. An event model that reproduces the measured RTL law projects (Fig.~\ref{fig:lanes}) \LaneTwoMpix\,Mpixel/s at 100\,MHz for two lanes (\LaneTwoLut{} LUTs, \LaneTwoDsp{} DSPs) and \LaneFourMpix\,Mpixel/s for four lanes fed two pixels per clock (\LaneFourLut{} LUTs, \LaneFourDsp{} DSPs).

\label{fig:lanesrtl}

\textit{The lanes in RTL.} We then built the lanes (contract v3): every MCU row is a restart segment; a dealer sends row $r$ to lane $r \bmod L$; each lane is the verified pipeline with a front end that takes two pixels per clock from a 64-bit stream, DC predictors and bit packer that restart at every segment, and a keystream that restarts under the per-frame segment key with the segment index as nonce; a merger writes the segments back in order with their restart markers, and the tag unit authenticates the merged scan. The output is bit-exact with the golden model for $L=1$, 2 and 4, in every protection level, under random stalls of both DMA streams and for frames with fewer MCU rows than lanes, and a 640$\times$480 frame has the same bytes for every $L$. After place and route at 100\,MHz, two lanes take \TwoLanesLuts{} LUTs, \TwoLanesBram{} BRAM36 and \TwoLanesDsp{} DSPs (WNS $\TwoLanesWns$\,ns) and four lanes \FourLanesLuts{} LUTs, \FourLanesBram{} BRAM36 and \FourLanesDsp{} DSPs ($\FourLanesWns$\,ns, lines up to 1024 pixels); the two-lane PYNQ-Z2 system with a 64-bit DMA closes timing with \OverlayLanesLuts{} LUTs (WNS $\OverlayLanesWns$\,ns). Each lane carries its own DCT and line buffers, so area grows with $L$; the lanes buy throughput, not efficiency. Measured at 100\,MHz (Fig.~\ref{fig:lanesrtl}), two lanes reach \RtlTwoLanesMpix\,Mpixel/s (\RtlTwoLanesCpp{} cycles per pixel) and four lanes \RtlFourLanesMpix\,Mpixel/s (\RtlFourLanesCpp), \RtlFourLanesSpeedup$\times$ the single pipeline, or \RtlFourLanesFps{} frames/s at 640$\times$480; the same pixel rate corresponds to \RtlFourLanesHdFps{} frames/s at 1080p. That is above the \mbox{150\,Mpixel/s} the commercial encoder of~\cite{logijpge} reports at 150\,MHz without any cryptography, at two thirds of its clock. The measurements fall below the projections (\ProjTwoLanesMpix{} and \ProjFourLanesMpix) mainly because those let the next frame's first MCU row arrive while the current frame is still coded; the RTL starts a frame only after the previous one is done, so every frame pays for its first row once, and each segment adds a keystream restart and a byte flush. The latency from the first pixel to the first scan word is \RtlFourLanesLatUs\,$\mu$s (\RtlFourLanesLatLines{} lines of a 640$\times$480 frame) for any number of lanes: it is the 16 lines of the first MCU row plus the pipeline.

\textit{Bitrate regulation.} A sender on a shared link must follow the available bandwidth. \textit{Change-driven coding.} Coding only the 16$\times$16 blocks (Fig.~\ref{fig:change}) that changed, as a retina does, needs \CdBytesFixed\,\% of the bytes on the fixed camera but \CdBytesMin--\CdBytesMax\,\% on the real clips, and the number of coded blocks tracks motion (correlation \CdCountFixed{} and \CdCountMin--\CdCountMax) even when the change map is hidden. Coding a constant 25\,\% of the blocks per frame, refreshing the oldest unchanged ones, and padding every frame hides both, at \CdPadFixed\,\% of the bytes and SSIM \CdPadSsimFixed{} on the fixed camera, but SSIM falls to \CdPadSsimMin--\CdPadSsimMax{} on edited clips.

\textit{Keyed block transforms.} Independent keyed flips per block defeat jigsaw solvers for block-scrambling ciphers. For the DCT, a flip is a pattern of sign changes, which E5 already encrypts: on \BtSources{} sources the jigsaw solver stays at chance (at most \BtNbMax\,\% neighbours) with or without keyed flips, and even with every tile position given, SSIM changes by at most \BtSsimDelta. E5's protection therefore rests on the block positions; an attacker who knew them would reach SSIM \BtOracleMin--\BtOracleMax{} on the real clips (\BtOracleFixed{} on the synthetic scene).

Four limitations bound our claims. First, our hardware numbers are post-route and the power figure is a vectorless estimate; \ifextended the complete Zynq systems close timing at 100\,MHz, but measurements on the board (frame rate including DMA and DDR, power) are pending, in particular whether DDR feeds the four-lane encoder its \RtlFourLanesInMBs\,MB/s of pixels\else board measurements (DMA, DDR, power) are pending\fi, and E5 exists only in the golden model. Second, sign and amplitude encryption leak outlines by construction, however many bits are encrypted; only a change of block positions removes them, at a bitrate cost. Third, encryption hides content, not length: frame sizes reveal motion at every level alike\ifextended{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:size})\else~\cite{wampler2015}\fi. They also identify a known video~\cite{schuster2017}: ten frame sizes pick the right one of \FpVideos{} videos \FpEtwoTen\,\% of the time under E1--E4 and \FpEfiveTen\,\% under E5 (chance \FpChance\,\%)\ifextended; padding to 1\,KB steps lowers that to \FpKbTen\,\% for $+$\FpKbCost\,\% bytes but not over 100 frames (\FpKbHundred\,\%), padding to the running maximum to \FpRunTen\,\% for $+$\FpRunCost\,\%, and only constant-size frames reach chance, at $+$\FpConstCost\,\% on these edited clips (Table~\ref{tab:fingerprint})\fi. Constant-size frames close the channel, cheaply for a fixed camera but at up to $+$\PadFilmMax\,\% bytes on edited clips; padding to the largest frame so far gives \PadRunSep{} for $+$\PadRunCost\,\% on the fixed camera (\PadRunFilmSepMin--\PadRunFilmSepMax{} on the real clips), while bitrate regulation lets up to \RcPadOverMax\,\% of frames exceed a cap. Fourth, our attackers are greedy or trained on a few clips; stronger puzzle solvers or attackers trained on large video corpora may extract more from E5, and a trained network already undoes sign-only encryption (E1). On the protocol side, the receiver cannot tell heavy benign loss from partial jamming, so only near-total suppression escalates, and the reaction time is bounded below by the one-second decision window.

\ifextended
\textit{Reproducibility and data.} Every number in this paper is produced by a script from stored results and inserted into the text by a generator, never typed; a test fails if a table in the manuscript differs from what the data produce. The open sources (the Blender Foundation films and the Kodak images) can be downloaded and rerun; the private clips are identified by SHA-256 hash and never shown, and frames of them are never stored as images. The golden model, the RTL, the testbenches, the experiment scripts and the scripts that rerun all GPU and long-CPU experiments in one cloud session are available in our repository.

\textit{Lessons.} Three kinds of error recurred while building this system, and each changed how we work. Simulation-only success hid timing problems that only place and route exposed, so post-route numbers are now part of every hardware claim. Experiment scripts were wrong more often than the RTL: a set point that clips opening on black could not reach, an estimator that wound up when its input saturated, a controller tuned at one operating point and unstable at another, and a test source that stamped the wall-clock time into every frame. Each was found because a result looked implausible on new data, and each now has a regression test. Finally, two claims had to be withdrawn when stronger evidence arrived: the E5 edge correlation of the U-Net is above that of the plain prior once more data is used, which the frame-statistics control explains, and the cost of hiding frame sizes is small only for fixed cameras.

\textit{Ethics.} The clips with identifiable people are used only to measure what an eavesdropper could recover; no picture derived from them appears here, and none is stored.

\section{Conclusion}
Encrypting coefficient signs and amplitudes with Ascon inside a JPEG encoder costs no cycles, keeps every protection level playable, and, combined with a per-frame tag over metadata and scan, turns a plain MJPEG stream into an authenticated one. Without a keyed change of block positions, however, an eavesdropper still sees the outline of the scene, and no level hides the frame sizes. A closed feedback loop answers active attacks within about a second in simulation. Next are on-board results on the PYNQ-Z2, macroblock shuffling and size padding in hardware, a shared Ascon core, and key derivation with the standard's extendable-output function.
